## Phase 2: Maths, Zipf by hand (about 2 hours)

Show every step:

1. Take this mini corpus: the, 12 times; a, 6 times; cat, 3 times; dog, 2 times; sits, 1 time. Sort by count. Rank 1 is the most frequent.
2. Rank 1: count 12. Rank 2: count 6. Rank 3: count 3. Rank 4: count 2. Rank 5: count 1.
3. Compute rank times count for every rank. What do you notice?
4. Check Zipf's formula: frequency proportional to 1 divided by rank to the power 1. Compare rank 1 vs rank 2: is the count ratio close to 2?
5. Least squares by hand: take two points, log10(count) and log10(rank): rank 1 count 12, rank 4 count 2. Slope = change in log(count) divided by change in log(rank). Compute it.
6. Write 2 sentences: what an exponent of 1.0 means, and what the article says about exceptions.

## Phase 3: Coding, ZIPF_CHECK.py (about 2-3 hours)

Reproduce Zipf's law on a real text:

1. Download an English text: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/11/pg11.txt (Alice in Wonderland). Save it as alice.txt.
2. Write a script, under 60 lines, stdlib only (math, collections).
3. Read alice.txt, split into words, count each word.
4. Sort by count, descending. Print the top 10 words with their counts and ranks.
5. For the top 10: print rank times count.
6. Estimate the exponent: least squares fit of log10(count) on log10(rank) over all words. Implement the fit yourself. Print the exponent, rounded to 3 decimals.
7. Compare with the article's claim, exponent near 1.0. Print the difference.
8. Print a verdict line: is Zipf's law a good fit on this text?

Save it as ZIPF_CHECK.py. Write the script yourself. Do not paste from any AI tool.
